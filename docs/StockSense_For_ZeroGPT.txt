STOCKSENSE AI: AN INTELLIGENT INVENTORY MANAGEMENT AND SUPPLIER DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEM FOR SMALL RETAIL STORES

ABSTRACT

Small retailers in India still manage inventory with notebooks and spreadsheets. They cannot see what will run out because they cannot afford enterprise software.

I built StockSense AI to solve this. It is an inventory manager and supplier decision-support tool. I used only free-tier cloud services to keep costs near zero.

The stack: React frontend, Supabase PostgreSQL, n8n workflows, and Groq Llama 3.1 8B. The system predicts stockout dates, marks at-risk items, scores suppliers using a Weighted Sum Model, and explains reorder decisions via the LLM. Alerts go to the shop owner's WhatsApp through Twilio.

A daily orchestration runs at 8:00 AM IST, executing eight workflows in sequence. I tested it with a catalog of six products. Production costs for a single store: under Rs. 500 per month. The owner gets timely alerts without touching anything.

Keywords: Inventory Management, Supply Chain Intelligence, Workflow Automation, n8n, Large Language Model, Supplier Scoring, Kirana Stores, SME Technology

INTRODUCTION

Over 12 million kirana stores operate across India. They are the backbone of neighborhood retail. Yet they struggle with basic inventory management. Manual tracking offers no way to predict what will sell. Owners guess, check stock randomly, or run out unexpectedly. When a product runs out, they lose customers. When they overstock, goods expire and money is wasted.

Small retailers face a specific set of constraints. Suppliers are scattered and unreliable. Delivery times change without warning. Affordable tools do not exist. Enterprise ERP costs thousands per month and is too complicated for one person to operate. Most point-of-sale systems only record what was sold. They do not predict what will be needed, warn about stockouts, rank suppliers, or recommend reorders.

I built StockSense AI to fill this gap. The system tracks inventory levels and projects stockout dates using daily sales velocity. It generates reorder suggestions using Groq Llama 3.1 8B. It ranks suppliers based on price, reliability, and delivery speed. It sends alerts directly to the shop owner's WhatsApp. And it runs on a nearly free infrastructure that requires no manual setup.

RELATED WORK

Research on inventory systems for small businesses has focused on various technologies: IoT sensors, RFID tags, cloud platforms. Most studies treat enterprise systems as the gold standard. However, uptake of these systems remains low in the small-business segment because enterprise solutions are expensive and difficult to use.

Within the realm of demand forecasting, machine learning approaches have emerged as a key capability. Research demonstrates that machine learning methods can achieve significantly better accuracy than simple baseline approaches such as moving averages. Seasonal factors and promotions also impact demand and must be incorporated for reliable forecasts. This forms a solid technical foundation, yet most implementations require skilled data engineers and infrastructure.

To address deployment complexity, recent research has explored large language models for business decision support. Large language models are now being applied to procurement and inventory decisions, as they can reduce human bias in reorder selections. However, applying LLMs to real-time operational workflows remains relatively unexplored, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Parallel to AI advances, the infrastructure layer has been democratized through low-code platforms. Low-code workflow platforms like n8n can dramatically reduce implementation time for small businesses, making complex automation accessible without requiring dedicated backend developers. This technological shift opens possibilities for businesses without substantial IT budgets.

On the supplier management side, multi-criteria evaluation methods have long been established in operations research. Supplier evaluation using methods such as the Weighted Sum Model---considering reliability, price, and delivery speed---has been studied extensively and is well-established in academic literature.

However, these individual components (demand forecasting, LLMs, workflow automation, supplier scoring) are rarely integrated into a coherent system designed specifically for resource-constrained retail environments. Indian retail presents unique constraints: the market is highly fragmented, products and demand patterns vary significantly by region, and few off-the-shelf solutions balance technical sophistication with operational simplicity and affordability.

Much published work on smart retail emphasizes IoT sensors and hardware-centric solutions, which require significant upfront capital investment---barcode readers, RFID equipment, network infrastructure---that small shops cannot afford. In contrast, software-only orchestration through low-code platforms offers an alternative architectural path: achieving intelligent automation without expensive sensors or dedicated infrastructure investments.

The proposed system builds upon these established research foundations while directly addressing the integration and affordability gap through a low-cost, WhatsApp-first, AI-enhanced solution architecture tailored to small Indian retailers.

MOTIVATION AND PROBLEM SPECIFICATION

While academic literature explores sophisticated inventory models, a significant gap exists between high-end enterprise solutions and the operational reality of small-scale Indian retailers.

Socio-Economic Motivation

Kirana stores serve over 50 percent of India's retail market and are essential to neighborhood commerce. Yet they face significant technology access challenges, particularly given their limited capital budgets and lack of technical staff. The primary objective of StockSense AI is to enable small business owners to access inventory intelligence without requiring expensive ERP subscriptions. The financial impact of a single stockout (lost customer) or overstock (expired goods) can significantly impact the thin operating margins of a neighborhood shop.

Gap Analysis and Technical Advantage

A comparative analysis reveals two major gaps in existing systems:

1. Financial Barrier: Most "smart" systems require hardware (RFID) or monthly SaaS fees (Rs. 500--5,000). StockSense operates at under Rs. 500/month by orchestrating free-tier and low-cost cloud services, compared to Rs. 4,000+ for enterprise alternatives.

2. Intelligent Support: Basic POS systems track what was sold, but do not predict what will be needed. The integration of 8B-parameter LLMs provides reasoning-based reorder logic that was previously only available to large-scale retail chains.

Problem Statement

The objective of this research is to design and implement a minimal-infrastructure, automated intelligence pipeline that transforms raw inventory data into actionable reorder recommendations and delivers them via WhatsApp, thereby reducing manual error and stockout risks in small retail environments.

PROPOSED SYSTEM

System Architecture

The system is organized in four layers, each with distinct responsibilities and communicating through well-defined REST interfaces. This layered design ensures modularity, scalability, and ease of testing.

The Frontend Layer is a React 19 Single Page Application (SPA), built with Vite 8 and styled using Tailwind CSS v4. It provides the shop owner with three primary interfaces: a real-time inventory dashboard displaying current stock levels and health scores, an AI reorder recommendations module for reviewing and confirming system-generated suggestions, and a supplier analytics interface for monitoring supplier performance and cost trends. The frontend communicates with the workflow engine via REST webhooks and interacts directly with Supabase for authentication and real-time data subscriptions.

The Workflow Engine Layer is implemented in n8n, an open-source workflow automation platform. Eight specialized workflows orchestrate the entire business logic pipeline. The workflows are structured as follows: WF-01 (Data Ingestion) normalizes incoming inventory updates and delivery records into the database. WF-02 (Stockout Calculation) applies the stockout prediction model to compute days-to-stockout for each product. WF-03 (Alert Filtering) identifies products that have crossed predefined risk thresholds. WF-04 (Risk Scoring) classifies products into HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW, or UNKNOWN risk categories. WF-05 (AI Suggestions) invokes the Groq API to generate natural-language reorder recommendations. WF-06 (Supplier Scoring) computes composite supplier scores using the Weighted Sum Model. WF-07 (WhatsApp Delivery) formats alerts and sends them to the shop owner via Twilio's WhatsApp Business API. Finally, WF-08 (Master Orchestrator) is a daily scheduled trigger (8:00 AM IST) that executes workflows WF-01 through WF-07 in strict sequential order, ensuring data consistency across all downstream operations.

The Database Layer uses Supabase, a managed PostgreSQL service offering real-time database subscriptions via WebSocket. The schema comprises eight relational tables: Products, InventoryTransactions, Suppliers, StockAlerts, ReorderSuggestions, SupplierScores, DeliveryHistory, and SystemLogs. All tables include a store_id foreign key to support future multi-store expansion. Real-time subscriptions push inventory and alert updates to the frontend the moment they are recorded, eliminating stale data and ensuring the dashboard reflects the current system state.

The AI/External Services Layer integrates Groq's hosted Llama 3.1 8B model for reorder reasoning and Twilio's WhatsApp Business API for alert delivery. Workflow WF-05 passes structured product data, current stock levels, and supplier availability to Groq via REST, receiving natural-language rationales for reorder suggestions. These explanations help shop owners understand the reasoning behind each recommendation, building trust in the system. Workflow WF-07 formats alerts with product names, stockout dates, and suggested order quantities, then delivers them via WhatsApp, ensuring the shop owner receives critical notifications in their preferred communication channel.

Communication between layers occurs exclusively through HTTP REST interfaces and standard database drivers. This architecture decouples components, allowing independent testing and future replacement of any layer without affecting others.

Terminology Definitions

To avoid ambiguity, we define two central concepts in the system:

Stock Alert: A Stock Alert is triggered automatically when a product's current inventory level falls to or below its configured reorder threshold. For example, if rice is configured with a reorder threshold of 20 units and current stock drops to 20 or fewer units, a Stock Alert is generated. This is a deterministic, threshold-based trigger with no AI involvement.

Reorder Suggestion: A Reorder Suggestion is an AI-generated recommendation for the shop owner, created after a Stock Alert is triggered. The Groq Llama 3.1 8B model receives product details, current stock levels, supplier information, and recent sales history, then outputs natural language reasoning explaining why and how much to reorder. The suggestion includes specific supplier recommendations ranked by the Weighted Sum Model and includes context such as estimated delivery times and cost implications.

In the workflows: WF-03 generates Stock Alerts; WF-05 generates Reorder Suggestions based on those alerts.

CONCLUSION

This paper presented StockSense AI, a system for inventory management and supplier selection in small Indian retail stores. I demonstrated that sophisticated inventory intelligence is possible using entirely free-tier and low-cost cloud services, making advanced supply chain tools accessible to retailers who cannot afford enterprise software costing Rs. 4,000 or more per month.

The system successfully integrates five key technical components: automated stockout prediction using daily sales velocity, AI-driven reorder recommendations via Groq Llama 3.1 8B, multi-criteria supplier scoring using a Weighted Sum Model, real-time WhatsApp alerts via Twilio, and fully orchestrated workflows in n8n. All of this operates at under Rs. 500 per month---an order of magnitude lower than traditional enterprise alternatives.

From an implementation perspective, the choice of low-code orchestration over traditional backend development proved particularly valuable. Rather than building custom microservices, n8n allowed rapid prototyping and deployment of complex business logic without requiring a dedicated development team. This design pattern is directly applicable to other small-business automation challenges where time-to-market and cost efficiency are critical.

However, the system has clear limitations that merit discussion. First, stockout prediction relies on historical daily sales velocity and assumes demand patterns remain relatively stable. Sudden seasonal spikes or regional disruptions would not be captured without additional contextual inputs. Second, the Weighted Sum Model for supplier scoring is deterministic and does not learn from historical performance feedback. Over time, the weights could be adjusted based on actual delivery outcomes, creating an adaptive feedback loop. Third, the current implementation assumes a single store; scaling to multiple stores would require implementing row-level security and potentially partitioned data schemas in PostgreSQL.

The success of this project suggests that the gap between what is theoretically optimal in supply chain management and what is practically deployed in small businesses is not primarily a technological one---it is an economic one. When tools become affordable and operationally simple, adoption becomes feasible. Future work should explore how similar low-cost integration patterns could serve other aspects of small retail operations: demand forecasting with external market data, dynamic pricing based on competitor analysis, or predictive maintenance for point-of-sale systems.

FUTURE SCOPE

The current system is a working baseline. Future improvements are possible:

Barcode scanning would let users look up products with the camera instead of typing IDs. The schema already accommodates barcode strings as identifiers.

Voice input in regional languages---Telugu, Hindi, Marathi---would allow stock updates by speaking. The Web Speech API provides this natively in browsers.

Two-way WhatsApp would let store owners approve reorder suggestions by replying to alerts, eliminating the need to log into the dashboard.

The schema has fields for seasonal tracking. During festivals, a seasonal multiplier could adjust demand estimates for accuracy around Diwali, Holi, and harvest season.

Multi-store support through Supabase Row Level Security would let store chains use the system. The schema already includes store_id in all tables.

Purchase order generation would take approved suggestions and create formal orders with UPI payment links, completing the procurement cycle.
